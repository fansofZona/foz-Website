Expected Threat (xT) treats the pitch as a grid of cells and prices each cell
by how much goal probability it feeds on average. Our contribution isn't the
model — it's the data. We hand-tracked every pass, carry, and shot for one
conference season (28 games, ~74,000 events), then asked: *which sequences
actually manufacture danger, and how does that change by score state?*

The headline result below: average accumulated xT per possession plotted
against possession duration. Long possessions climb, but only in the opening
minutes. Past ~25 seconds the marginal second adds almost nothing — until a
shot finally happens, most of the value is in the first two thirds of a
touch.

That plateau is a coaching signal. Teams can spend 25 seconds and a dozen
passes to get back to the position a good first pass created in five. When we
ranked build-up patterns by xT per possession, direct entries into the final
third dominated — not because they are prettier, but because they reach the
dangerous cells before the block can slide.

If your conference doesn't publish tracking data, hand-charting works. It just
hurts. Everyone who touched a clipboard for this one can confirm.

The full model, hand-tracker, and cell weights are published on GitHub. The
caveat section is honest about how much depends on knowing *who* is on the
ball, which our grid model deliberately ignores.
