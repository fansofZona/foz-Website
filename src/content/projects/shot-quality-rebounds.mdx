Good looks and good luck are different revenue streams. We separated them by
fitting a model where each possession's scoring value splits into a
*repeatable* part (where the shot came from, who got it, how contested) and a
*lucky* part (who the rebound fell to). Three seasons of conference play gives
us enough noise to name the winner of each matchup.

The signature chart: expected offensive rebound rate per game against points
generated from those rebounds. The trend is real but shallow — rebounding is
worth its money, but it is satisfyingly-average and sticky when you need it most:

## The shrinkage saves us from overreading the leaderboard

Fourteen team-seasons is a tiny sample for a model with rebound-floor and
transition terms. A straight regression rearms every unlucky team with
confidence intervals the size of the arena. So we fit a hierarchical
multilevel model with partial pooling: each team-season's estimate pulls
toward the conference mean at a rate proportional to how little information it
carries.

The practical upshot: last year's conference runner-up looked like the best
offensive-rebounding team in the league by raw rate. After shrinkage, they
were merely good.

The Stan model, data, and a shot-by-shot "was that luck or skill" brief are
all open. Rebound variance is real — just not as loud as the color
commentator.
