The *why* is short: fourteen team-seasons is not enough data to rank
rebounding ability by raw rate. The *how* is a hierarchical model with
partial pooling, and this post walks through it the way we wish someone had
for us.

**The setup.** Each team-season has a true offensive rebounding ability
`θ`. The observed rate `y` is `θ` plus noise that shrinks as the number of
rebound opportunities grows. A flat model treats each season as its own
planet. A pooled model treats everyone as identical. Hierarchical shrinkage
sits between: every `θ` is drawn from a league-wide distribution, and the
posterior pulls each estimate toward the league mean in proportion to its
variance.

That means a 500-rebound team keeps nearly all of its raw rate, while an
18-game season's rebound rate is dragged most of the way home. This is the
behavior people accuse statistics of erasing — but erasing is the point:

**The prior is not opinion; it's the league.** The hierarchical prior is
estimated from the same data, so it encodes "college teams mostly look alike."
If a team truly is Alabama on the glass, its raw rate will be extreme enough
that the noise cannot drag it down — shrinkage only deregulates noise.

**The implementation.** One Stan program, weakly informative priors, four
chains, and a convergence diagnostic we actually read (all R-hat < 1.01). The
model file is 40 lines. The posterior is more interesting than the point
estimates: the 90% intervals on team ordering overlap all over the place,
which is exactly how we should talk about a 14-game sample.

**The honest tail.** Shrinkage does not rescue selection on the response. Our
leaderboard is sorted after fitting, and re-ranking a noisy distribution
always overstates the extremes. We report credible intervals alongside every
rank; you should demand them from anyone posting a per-game rebound chart in
March.
