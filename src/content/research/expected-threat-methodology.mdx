This is the methods note for our *Expected Threat in College Soccer* report.
It is the part we usually skip at the meeting and then regret skipping.

**The model.** The pitch is a 16×11 grid. Each cell gets a value equal to the
probability that a possession ending in that cell eventually produces a goal
within three actions — estimated with a one-step transition table, the way
Karun Singh's original xT assigns cell prices by "what happens next, on
average." A pass is worth the difference between the value of its destination
cell and its start cell. That difference, accumulated, is xT.

**The data.** No tracking feed exists for our conference, so we charted.
Two annotators, one template, a book of "if ambiguous, count as carry"
rules. Inter-rater agreement, measured on 500 shared events, was 0.91 for
event type and 0.83 for location. The locations are the loose thread in the
whole project; cell-level error washes out but pass-target error shows up in
the wings.

**The weight training.** Cell values are fit on the full 28-game season, then
cross-validated in five folds at the match level so consecutive possessions in
one game never leak into the same training fold.

We verified the standard sanity checks: team-level xT correlates with goals
(r = 0.82), and xT generated *against* a team correlates with expected
conceded (r = 0.79). The model does not predict shots, it prices them.

The one thing you should distrust about these numbers is how we handled
keeper involvement — long balls toward the box are priced by their landing
cell even when nobody is near it. Our sensitivity check below shows how the
season's ranking shifts as that assumption gets stricter:

**Reproduction.** The full pipeline — template, annotator rules, cell-weight
optimizer, and per-match holdouts — ships in the repo. If your league has
tracking data, skip the charting step and use the same cell weights; ours are
calibrated to ordinary broadcast-visible events, not to x/y tracking.

Send us your league's data and we'll run the same cross-validation. The
argument is the point; the numbers just have to be cheap to recheck.
