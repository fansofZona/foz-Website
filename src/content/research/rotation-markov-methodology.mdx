Volleyball's six rotations are a tiny, perfect Markov chain: your serve-receive
alignment defines the state, the rally resolves to a point or a sideout, and
you deterministically rotate. That structure means expected points per
rotation is not random — it is the stationary behavior of a system we can
measure.

**Stage 1: chart the film.** We coded every rally of three conference seasons
by the rotation it started in, the result, and who served. Three seasons, 200+
matches, about 41,000 rallies, two annotators, one rulebook.

**Stage 2: the transition matrix.** For each starting rotation `i`, we count
what happens next: point won (`i` scores), point lost (opponent scores), or
sideout (opponent wins the rally and *begins* their serve-receive in their
corresponding rotation). The result is a 6×6 matrix `P` where `P[i,j]` is the
probability of moving from your rotation `i` to opponent rotation `j` in one
rally, plus the absorption probabilities of scoring.

**Stage 3: solve the cycle.** If the chain is irreducible and aperiodic —
practical volleyball is — there is a unique stationary distribution `π` and a
well-defined expected points-per-rotation vector. The model's output,
rotation by rotation, is what we report elsewhere; here is the shape of *how
much* score depends on starting alignment:

**Why the chain is the right shape for this question.** A regression from
rotation → points would treat each rally as independent. The Markov framing
captures the mechanism: losing your serve to a bad rotation costs you the
point *and* hands the opponent their best serve-receive alignment. The
difference between playing your best passer alongside your best hitters and
spreading them out is exactly a cascade, not a sum.

**Validation.** We held out the final regular-season match for each team and
predicted points per set from the season's matrix. Mean absolute error was
0.7 points per set — better than the points share model, and it degraded
gracefully when we truncated the training data to ten matches.

**The caveat.** Our state machine has no notion of *serve order* (who serves
next is part of the state in real volleyball). Adding it doubles the matrix
size and buys maybe two percentage points of fit. We left it out, with
blessing of the model police, because the rotation finding survives it either
way.
