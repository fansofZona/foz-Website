Every spring a student sports website runs a ranking of conference teams using
circular reasoning and eighteen data points. Then the comments flame it. We'd
like to talk about the part that's actually fixable: the sample size.

Eighteen games is worse than it looks. A quarter of a season of basketball is
enough to sort the great from the bad, but it is nowhere near enough to sort
the good from the good. What most people do instead is rank, and rank noise.

The alternative is to not erase the small sample but to *partially believe it*.
A hierarchical model estimates every team's ability while pulling each estimate
toward the league average proportionally to how little information that team
carries. A 30-game team keeps most of its raw performance; the 18-game team
shrink, a lot.

Three things below the fold, because they matter academically:

**1. Shrinkage changes the leaderboard we obsess over.** Raw marks love a
hot start. Pulling toward the mean gives you a confidence interval around
"who's actually ahead," which is the honest answer to most rankings questions.

**2. It changes lineups, not just articles.** A coach with an 18-game cell
chart who trusts the raw rate will play the hot hand. A coach with a shrunken
estimate plays the one with the better ceiling — the difference is real and
free.

**3. It does not say "small data is useless."** The whole trick is that the
prior is not opinion; it's the rest of the league. You learn from your team
faster because you start from everyone else.

The model is a page of Stan plus a plot of the posteriors, and it's in the
repo for the shot-quality report. The comments can stay, but the rankings
don't have to swing with whoever won their last Wednesday.
